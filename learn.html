What is El Niño?

El Niño is a periodic warming of the ocean surface across the central and eastern tropical Pacific. It is one phase of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), a natural ocean–atmosphere cycle that NOAA considers one of the most influential drivers of climate on Earth. ENSO has three phases: the warm phase, El Niño; the cool phase, La Niña; and the in-between state, neutral.

How El Niño forms

In a typical year, trade winds blow from east to west along the equator, piling warm surface water up in the western Pacific near Indonesia and Australia. When those winds weaken, the warm water drifts back east toward South America, and the heat and moisture it releases shift tropical rainfall eastward with it.

The ocean and atmosphere have to change together for El Niño to take hold: warmer water alone is not enough unless the winds and rainfall respond as well. Eastward-moving pulses of warm water called Kelvin waves help carry heat across the basin. They start in the western Pacific, can stretch thousands of miles, and several may follow one another over the course of an event.

The ENSO cycle

El Niño returns irregularly, about every two to seven years, and an event usually lasts nine to twelve months. La Niña brings the opposite pattern: cooler-than-normal water in the eastern Pacific and stronger trade winds. Moving from one phase to the other can take anywhere from one to six months.

How it is measured

Scientists watch sea surface temperatures in the Niño 3.4 region of the central and eastern equatorial Pacific. NOAA's working definition of El Niño is water there running at least 0.5 °C above normal for five overlapping three-month periods in a row, together with weaker trade winds and an eastward shift in rainfall. Events are rated weak, moderate, strong, or very strong.

In February 2026, NOAA's Climate Prediction Center shifted to the Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI). Instead of comparing Niño 3.4 with a fixed 30-year average, as the older Oceanic Niño Index did, RONI compares it with the tropics as a whole. That makes it less sensitive to overall ocean warming and to the choice of baseline years.

Measurements come from satellites, ships, drifting floats, and buoys. The Tropical Atmosphere Ocean (TAO) array, about 70 moored buoys across the equatorial Pacific, was built after the 1982–83 El Niño caught forecasters off guard. Since 2014 it has been part of the wider Tropical Pacific Observing System, led by NOAA and Japan's JAMSTEC.

Impacts in the U.S.

El Niño's influence on the United States is strongest in winter. It pushes the Pacific jet stream south and east, steering more storms into California and across the southern states. A band from central California to Florida and up the East Coast tends to be wetter and cooler than normal, while the northern tier, out to the Great Lakes, tends to be warmer and drier. Stronger events make these outcomes more likely, but no two El Niños play out exactly alike.

Impacts around the world

Heavy rain and flooding often hit Peru, Ecuador, and Chile, while Australia, Indonesia, and parts of southern Asia face a higher risk of severe drought. Warm water fuels hurricanes in the central and eastern Pacific but tends to suppress them in the Atlantic. Off South America, fish move north or south in search of colder water, and in strong events the effects on marine life can reach as far as the Gulf of Alaska.

These shifts ripple into agriculture, wildfire risk, flooding, water supply, and food security, which is why ENSO matters for insurers, planners, and policymakers as well as scientists.

Forecasting

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center issues an official ENSO outlook on the second Thursday of every month, a consensus forecast built by a team of forecasters from ocean and atmosphere observations and computer models. Forecasting when an El Niño will begin is hard because several conditions have to line up. Once an event is underway, forecasters can predict its strength and evolution fairly well. Predictions made during the Northern Hemisphere spring are the least reliable, a challenge known as the "spring predictability barrier."

A brief history

Fishermen in Peru noticed the pattern centuries ago, when warm water and poor catches arrived around Christmas; the name El Niño refers to the Christ child. Clues preserved in coral, tree rings, ice cores, cave formations, and seafloor mud show that ENSO has been operating for a very long time. The 1982–83 and 1997–98 events were among the strongest of the 20th century.
