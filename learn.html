What is El Niño?

El Niño is a periodic warming of sea surface temperatures across the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean. In normal years, trade winds push warm surface water west toward Indonesia and Australia, and cold water wells up along South America. During El Niño, those winds weaken, warm water spreads east, and patterns of rainfall and atmospheric circulation shift with it.

The ENSO cycle

El Niño is the warm phase of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO). Its cool counterpart, La Niña, brings colder-than-normal water to the eastern Pacific and stronger trade winds. Events typically recur every two to seven years and usually last nine to twelve months, though some persist longer.

How it is measured

Scientists track ENSO with sea surface temperature anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region of the equatorial Pacific. NOAA’s Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) uses a three-month running mean of these anomalies: values of +0.5 °C or higher indicate El Niño conditions, and −0.5 °C or lower indicate La Niña. The Southern Oscillation Index, based on air-pressure differences between Tahiti and Darwin, captures the atmospheric side.

Impacts around the world

Because the tropical Pacific is so large, shifts in its heat and rainfall ripple through the global atmosphere. El Niño is often associated with wetter winters in the southern United States, drought in Indonesia and Australia, weaker Atlantic hurricane seasons, and reduced anchovy catches off Peru. These effects reach agriculture, energy demand, insurance losses, and public health.

Forecasting

Forecasters combine ocean buoys, satellites, and climate models to predict ENSO several months ahead. Predictions are least reliable through the northern spring, a period known as the “spring predictability barrier.”
