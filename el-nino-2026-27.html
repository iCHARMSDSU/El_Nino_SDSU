Why this El Niño stands out

Compared with every El Niño since 1950 at the same stage of development, the lab's analysis of NOAA data finds that the 2026/27 event has:

  1. the warmest Niño 3.4 sea surface temperatures for this point in an event,
  2. warmed faster since January than any past event,
  3. reached record September values in all four Niño regions,
  4. record heat stored in the upper ocean,
  5. a record volume of water warmer than 28 °C, and
  6. a forecast peak that would set a new record.

Sea surface temperature

The surface signal is the one most people recognize: a tongue of unusually warm water stretching east along the equator toward South America. Maps by month show how far and how fast that warmth has spread, and how its pattern compares with past events.

Heat below the surface

Much of an El Niño's fuel sits below the surface. Warm water in the top few hundred meters of the Pacific builds up before an event and then spreads east. How much warm water is stored is one of the best early signs of how strong an event may become.

Kelvin waves and sea level

Bursts of westerly wind in the western Pacific set off Kelvin waves, slow pulses of warm water that travel east along the equator over a couple of months. Because warm water expands, each pulse raises sea level slightly as it passes, which lets satellites track it from space.

What comes next

NOAA expects this El Niño to keep strengthening through the end of 2026 and to persist into early 2027. Strong events usually peak in late fall or early winter, then fade during the following spring, sometimes flipping into La Niña within a year.
